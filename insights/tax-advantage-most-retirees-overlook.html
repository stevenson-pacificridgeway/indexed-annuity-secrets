The tax advantage most retirees overlook

By Gregory Stevenson · 2 min read

Most people spend their working years focused on one tax strategy: put as much as possible into a 401(k) or IRA to reduce this year's tax bill.

That's not a bad strategy while you're earning. But in retirement, it can create a problem nobody warned you about: nearly everything you withdraw is taxable.

How it usually plays out

You retire with most of your money in traditional (pre-tax) accounts. Every dollar you pull out gets taxed as ordinary income. Add Social Security benefits — up to 85% of which can also be taxable — and your effective tax rate in retirement may be higher than you expected.

Then at 73, Required Minimum Distributions kick in. Whether you need the money or not, you have to take it. And pay taxes on it.

I've sat across from people who said, "I thought I was done worrying about taxes." They're not. They just traded one tax problem for another.

This is where tax diversification comes in

The idea is simple: have money in different "tax buckets" so you can control how much taxable income you create each year.

  • Taxable accounts (brokerage, bank): Taxed on gains and interest annually
  • Tax-deferred accounts (401k, IRA): Taxed when you withdraw
  • Tax-advantaged vehicles (Roth, certain annuities): May offer tax-deferred growth or tax-free income depending on the structure

Non-qualified annuities, for example, grow tax-deferred. You don't pay taxes on the growth until you take it out. And depending on how you structure the income, you may have more control over your annual tax bill than with a required distribution from an IRA.

This isn't about avoiding taxes. It's about having options so you can choose when and how you pay them.

The people who plan for this in advance tend to keep more of their money. The ones who don't often end up surprised by their tax bill in year 3 or 4 of retirement. This is educational information, not tax advice — bring these questions to your CPA.
