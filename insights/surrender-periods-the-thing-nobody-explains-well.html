Surrender periods: the thing nobody explains well

By Gregory Stevenson · 2 min read

If there's one thing that gives people pause about annuities, it's the surrender period. And I get it — "you can't touch your money for X years" sounds limiting.

But the reality is more nuanced than most people realize, and I think it deserves a straight explanation.

What is a surrender period?

When you put money into a deferred annuity, the insurance company commits to a set of contractual terms — interest rates, income options, guarantees. In return, they ask that you keep the money in place for a certain number of years. That period is the surrender period.

If you take out more than the allowed amount during that window, you pay a penalty called a surrender charge. The charge is highest in the first year and typically decreases over time until it reaches zero.

Why does it exist?

Insurance companies need time to earn the returns that fund your benefits. They're making long-term commitments to you, so they need a long-term commitment in return. It's the trade-off that makes the math work.

Think of it like a CD at a bank. You get a better rate because you agree to keep the money in place. Early withdrawal means a penalty. Same concept, different product.

What most people miss

  • Free withdrawal provisions: Most annuities allow you to withdraw 10% of your account value each year — no penalty. That's real liquidity.
  • Nursing home waivers: Many contracts waive surrender charges entirely if you're confined to a nursing home or long-term care facility.
  • Terminal illness provisions: Similar to nursing home waivers — many contracts provide access to your full account value.
  • Surrender periods vary: Some are 3 years. Some are 10. And shorter surrender periods often come with different rate trade-offs. It's not one-size-fits-all.

The question worth asking

Rather than "can I get my money back," consider: "How much of my total portfolio do I actually need liquid access to, and how much am I comfortable letting work for me?"

For most people, the answer isn't "all of it." And the portion you don't need immediate access to? That's where an annuity may do its best work.
