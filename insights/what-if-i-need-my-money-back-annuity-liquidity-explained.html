"What if I need my money back?" Annuity liquidity, explained

By Gregory Stevenson · 2 min read

If there's one question I hear more than any other, it's this: "What happens if I put money into an annuity and then I need it back?"

It's a fair question. And the honest answer is: it depends on the product, but the picture is usually better than people expect.

Start with the part most people worry about: surrender periods

Yes, most deferred annuities have a window — typically 5 to 10 years — where there's a charge if you withdraw more than the allowed amount. That's real, and it's something you should understand before you commit.

But here's what often gets left out of that conversation:

Most products include a free withdrawal provision. That typically means you can pull out up to 10% of your account value each year with no penalty. If you have $300,000 in an annuity, that's up to $30,000 per year — accessible without a charge.

Many products include hardship waivers. If you're diagnosed with a terminal illness or need nursing home care, many contracts allow full access to your funds. The specifics vary by product, but these provisions exist in a large number of contracts.

Surrender charges decrease over time. A product with a 7-year surrender period might charge 7% in year one, 6% in year two, and so on — dropping to zero. After the surrender period ends, your money is fully accessible.

The real question isn't "can I get my money back?"

It's: "How much of my money do I truly need liquid — available at a moment's notice — and how much can I afford to put to work for my future?"

Most people don't need 100% of their money accessible on day one. They need enough for emergencies, short-term expenses, and peace of mind. The rest can be positioned to work harder — potentially generating income or growth.

A client I spoke with had this exact concern. He valued liquidity above almost everything else. When we looked at his actual cash flow needs, he realized he could comfortably allocate a portion of his assets toward a longer-term strategy while keeping plenty available for anything life might throw at him.

The liquidity question is one of the most important ones to get right.
